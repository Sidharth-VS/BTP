## Cardiovascular Disease and Acute Coronary Syndromes

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a class of diseases that involve the heart or blood vessels[cite: 39]. It is the leading cause of death globally, accounting for approximately 17.9 million deaths per year[cite: 39]. CVD includes coronary artery disease, heart failure, stroke, and peripheral artery disease[cite: 39].

The primary underlying mechanism is atherosclerosis: a chronic inflammatory process wherein low-density lipoprotein (LDL) particles infiltrate endothelial layers, oxidize, and attract macrophages. These form lipid-laden foam cells that coalesce into fatty streaks and fibrous atherosclerotic plaques. Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) arises when unstable plaques rupture, exposing prothrombotic elements that precipitate rapid platelet aggregation and occlusive thrombus formation.

ACS stratifies into unstable angina, Non-ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction (NSTEMI), and ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction (STEMI), differentiated via 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECG) and cardiac troponin (cTnI/cTnT) laboratory assays. In heart failure, pathology separates into heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF, systolic pump impairment with LVEF under 40%) and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF, diastolic ventricular relaxation stiffness). B-type Natriuretic Peptide (BNP) serves as a key diagnostic biomarker for myocardial wall stress.

Risk factors for cardiovascular disease include high blood pressure (hypertension), high cholesterol, smoking, diabetes, obesity, physical inactivity, and family history[cite: 39]. Many of these risk factors can be managed through lifestyle changes and medication[cite: 39].

## Hypertension (High Blood Pressure) and Hemodynamics

Hypertension is defined as consistently elevated blood pressure — specifically a systolic pressure of 130 mmHg or higher, or a diastolic pressure of 80 mmHg or higher (per the 2017 ACC/AHA guidelines)[cite: 39]. Elevated blood pressure exerts chronic shear stress against arterial walls, causing systemic endothelial dysfunction, microvascular rarefaction, and compensatory left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH).

Primary (essential) hypertension has no identifiable cause and develops gradually over years, driven by polygenic susceptibility and lifestyle variables[cite: 39]. Secondary hypertension is caused by an underlying condition such as renal artery stenosis, chronic kidney disease, adrenal gland tumors (pheochromocytoma, primary aldosteronism), or thyroid problems[cite: 39].

The Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS) regulates vascular resistance and volume balance. Diminished renal perfusion triggers renin secretion, which cleaves angiotensinogen into angiotensin I. Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme (ACE) converts this into angiotensin II, a potent vasoconstrictor that also stimulates aldosterone release from the adrenal cortex, driving sodium and water retention.

Treatment options include[cite: 39]:
- Lifestyle modifications: DASH diet, regular aerobic exercise, limiting sodium to under 2,300 mg daily, reducing alcohol[cite: 39]
- First-line medications: ACE inhibitors (e.g., lisinopril), Angiotensin II Receptor Blockers (ARBs, e.g., losartan), Dihydropyridine Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs, e.g., amlodipine), and Thiazide diuretics (e.g., chlorthalidone)[cite: 39]
- Second-line additions: Beta-blockers (e.g., metoprolol, carvedilol) for concurrent ischemic heart disease or heart failure, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (spironolactone)[cite: 39]

Hypertensive crises (systolic over 180 mmHg or diastolic over 120 mmHg) are categorized as hypertensive urgency (asymptomatic) or hypertensive emergency (presenting acute end-organ damage such as aortic dissection, encephalopathy, or pulmonary edema), requiring intravenous titrations of nicardipine or labetalol.

## Diabetes Mellitus and Metabolic Pathophysiology

Diabetes mellitus is a group of diseases that result in too much sugar in the blood (high blood glucose)[cite: 39]. Type 1 diabetes is caused by an autoimmune reaction that destroys beta cells in the pancreas, leading to absolute insulin deficiency[cite: 39]. Type 2 diabetes occurs when peripheral tissues develop insulin resistance, and pancreatic beta cells progressively fail to produce sufficient compensatory insulin to maintain normal blood sugar levels[cite: 39].

Under healthy physiology, insulin binding to tyrosine kinase receptors triggers intracellular phosphorylation cascades that translocate GLUT4 glucose transporters to the plasma membrane in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue. Impaired insulin signaling causes uninhibited hepatic gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis, worsening systemic hyperglycemia.

HbA1c (glycated hemoglobin) is a blood test that measures average blood glucose over the past 2-3 months by assessing non-enzymatic glycation of hemoglobin[cite: 39]. Normal: below 5.7%[cite: 39]. Prediabetes: 5.7-6.4%[cite: 39]. Diabetes: 6.5% or higher (or fasting plasma glucose of 126 mg/dL or greater)[cite: 39].

Acute metabolic complications include Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA, predominantly in Type 1), driven by uninhibited lipolysis into free fatty acids that liver mitochondria convert into acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate, and Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS, in Type 2), presenting severe hyperosmolar dehydration. Long-term microvascular and macrovascular complications include peripheral diabetic neuropathy, nephropathy (diabetic kidney disease), retinopathy, and accelerated cardiovascular disease[cite: 39]. Pharmacotherapy spans metformin (biguanide), SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, promoting urinary glucose excretion), GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, enhancing incretin response), and exogenous insulin regimens.

## Cancer Biology and Molecular Oncology

Cancer is a disease in which some of the body's cells grow uncontrollably and spread to other parts of the body[cite: 39]. Cancer can start almost anywhere in the human body, which is made up of trillions of cells[cite: 39]. Malignant transformation involves cumulative mutations in proto-oncogenes (gain-of-function mutations driving autonomy, e.g., KRAS, MYC, HER2) and tumor suppressor genes (loss-of-function mutations disabling surveillance, e.g., TP53, RB1, BRCA1/2).

The hallmarks of cancer include[cite: 39]:
- Sustaining proliferative signaling[cite: 39]
- Evading growth suppressors[cite: 39]
- Resisting cell death (apoptosis)[cite: 39]
- Enabling replicative immortality (telomerase activation)[cite: 39]
- Inducing angiogenesis (VEGF-mediated neovascularization)[cite: 39]
- Activating invasion and metastasis[cite: 39]
- Reprogramming energy metabolism (the Warburg effect: aerobic glycolysis)
- Evading immune destruction

Immunotherapy has revolutionized cancer treatment[cite: 39]. Checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1/PD-L1, anti-CTLA-4) release brakes on the immune system, allowing T cells to attack cancer cells more effectively[cite: 39]. Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy genetically engineers patient T cells to express synthetic receptors targeting tumor cell antigens (e.g., CD19 in hematologic malignancies). Targeted therapies apply small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors (imatinib, osimertinib) or monoclonal antibodies (trastuzumab) to disrupt oncogenic signaling pathways.

## Infectious Diseases and Immunology

Infectious diseases are caused by pathogenic microorganisms — bacteria, viruses, parasites, or fungi[cite: 39]. They can spread, directly or indirectly, from one person to another[cite: 39]. 

Bacteria are classified by Gram-staining based on cell wall composition. Gram-positive organisms possess thick peptidoglycan layers that retain crystal violet dye, while Gram-negative organisms possess a thinner peptidoglycan layer enveloped by an outer membrane rich in lipopolysaccharide (endotoxin), triggering intense inflammatory cascades and septic shock upon bloodstream invasion. Viruses act as obligate intracellular parasites containing RNA or DNA genomes that hijack host ribosome translation mechanisms to assemble new virions.

Antibiotic resistance is one of the biggest public health challenges[cite: 39]. When bacteria change in response to antibiotics, the drugs become less effective[cite: 39]. Mechanisms of resistance include enzymatic degradation (beta-lactamases), target site modification (mutated penicillin-binding proteins in MRSA), efflux pump upregulation, and decreased membrane porin permeability. MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) are examples of drug-resistant bacteria[cite: 39].

Vaccines work by training the immune system to recognize and combat pathogens[cite: 39]. mRNA vaccines (like those for COVID-19) deliver genetic instructions for producing a protein that triggers an immune response, without using live virus[cite: 39]. Upon lipid nanoparticle cell delivery, host ribosomes translate viral spike proteins to stimulate both humoral antibodies (B cells) and cell-mediated immunity (CD4+ helper and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells).

## Mental Health and Neurobiology

Mental health includes emotional, psychological, and social well-being[cite: 39]. It affects how we think, feel, and act and helps determine how we handle stress, relate to others, and make choices[cite: 39].

Depression is a common and serious medical illness that negatively affects feelings, thoughts, and actions[cite: 39]. Major depressive disorder (MDD) is characterized by persistent depressed mood or loss of interest in activities (anhedonia), causing significant impairment[cite: 39]. The monoamine hypothesis posits that depressive pathophysiology involves functional deficits in serotonergic, noradrenergic, and dopaminergic neurotransmission in cortical and limbic structures. Chronic neuroinflammation and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation lead to elevated cortisol, suppressing Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) and promoting hippocampal atrophy.

Bipolar disorder involves cycling between major depressive episodes and manic or hypomanic states characterized by grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, and flight of ideas, requiring mood stabilizers (lithium, valproic acid) rather than antidepressant monotherapy.

Treatment includes[cite: 39]:
- Psychotherapy: CBT (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy), DBT (Dialectical Behavior Therapy)[cite: 39]
- Medications: SSRIs (e.g., sertraline, escitalopram), SNRIs (e.g., venlafaxine, duloxetine), atypical antidepressants (bupropion, mirtazapine)[cite: 39]
- Neuromodulation: Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT) and Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) for treatment-resistant major depression
- Combination approaches are often most effective[cite: 39]

## Pharmacology Basics and Pharmacodynamics

Pharmacology analyzes interactions between living organisms and chemical substances that alter biochemical function.

Pharmacokinetics describes what the body does to a drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME)[cite: 39]. The half-life of a drug is the time it takes for the drug's concentration in the body to be reduced by half[cite: 39]. Bioavailability (F) represents the fraction of an administered dose reaching systemic circulation intact (100% for IV administration). Volume of Distribution (Vd) relates total drug mass to observed plasma concentration; a high Vd denotes extensive lipid-soluble tissue sequestration. Hepatic metabolism occurs via Phase I oxidation, reduction, and hydrolysis reactions (predominantly mediated by the Cytochrome P450 enzyme family, such as CYP3A4 and CYP2D6) and Phase II conjugation (glucuronidation) to promote renal elimination.

Pharmacodynamics describes what a drug does to the body: mechanism of action, dose-response relationships, and therapeutic effects[cite: 39]. Drug-receptor interactions are central to pharmacodynamics[cite: 39]. Agonists bind receptors to elicit maximal intrinsic responses; partial agonists evoke submaximal activation. Antagonists block receptor signaling: competitive antagonists displace agonist dose-response curves to the right without lowering maximal efficacy ($E_{max}$), whereas non-competitive antagonists irreversibly bind allosteric sites, depressing $E_{max}$.

The therapeutic index (TI) = LD50 / ED50, where LD50 is the lethal dose for 50% and ED50 is the effective dose for 50% of subjects[cite: 39]. A high TI means the drug is relatively safe[cite: 39]. Narrow therapeutic index agents (e.g., warfarin, digoxin, lithium) demand continuous therapeutic drug monitoring to prevent severe toxicity.

## Renal Physiology and Fluid Homeostasis

The kidneys maintain systemic homeostasis by regulating blood volume, arterial pressure, electrolyte concentrations, and blood acid-base equilibrium. Each human kidney contains approximately one million nephrons.

Glomerular filtration occurs across a tri-layer filtration barrier: fenestrated capillary endothelium, the glomerular basement membrane, and podocyte foot processes. The Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR, normal ~90-120 mL/min/1.73m²) acts as the primary index of renal function. Filtrate undergoes targeted modification across distinct nephron segments:
- Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT): Reabsorbs roughly 65% of filtered water, sodium, and chloride, alongside 100% of filtered glucose and amino acids via sodium-dependent symporters.
- Loop of Henle: Generates hyperosmotic medullary gradients via countercurrent multiplication. The descending limb is water-permeable; the thick ascending limb actively pumps sodium, potassium, and chloride ($Na^+/2Cl^-/K^+$ cotransporter, targeted by loop diuretics like furosemide).
- Distal Convoluted Tubule and Collecting Duct: Fine-tune sodium and water excretion under the influence of aldosterone (increasing epithelial sodium channels) and Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH/Vasopressin, inserting aquaporin-2 water channels).
