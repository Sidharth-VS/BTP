## Stock Market Fundamentals

A stock represents ownership in a company and constitutes a claim on part of the company's assets and earnings[cite: 37]. Stocks are also called shares or equity[cite: 37]. When a company needs to raise capital, it can issue shares of stock through an initial public offering (IPO)[cite: 37]. Following the primary issuance, shares trade in secondary markets among institutional and retail participants.

Trading execution relies on continuous double auction order books managed by designated market makers and electronic communication networks (ECNs). Market participants place market orders for immediate execution at the prevailing ask or bid, or limit orders to specify an execution price boundary. Market liquidity refers to the ease with which large transaction volumes can be cleared without causing substantial price slippage. Secondary market mechanisms also allow short selling, wherein an investor borrows shares to sell immediately, intending to repurchase them at a lower price to profit from downward price discovery.

The stock market refers to the collection of markets and exchanges where the buying, selling, and issuance of shares of publicly held companies take place[cite: 37]. The New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and NASDAQ are the two largest stock exchanges in the world by market capitalization[cite: 37].

## Portfolio Diversification and Modern Portfolio Theory

Diversification is a risk management strategy that mixes a wide variety of investments within a portfolio[cite: 37]. The rationale behind this technique is that a portfolio constructed of different kinds of assets will, on average, yield higher long-term returns and lower the risk of any individual holding or security[cite: 37].

Under Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), total investment risk decomposes into systematic risk (market-wide volatility driven by macroeconomic variables) and unsystematic risk (idiosyncratic company or industry risk). Unsystematic risk can be diversified away toward zero by combining assets with low or negative correlation coefficients (-1.0 to +1.0). The Markowitz Efficient Frontier represents the optimal set of portfolios offering the maximum expected return for a defined variance level, or the lowest variance for a targeted return.

A well-diversified portfolio includes stocks across different sectors (technology, healthcare, energy, consumer goods), different geographic regions (domestic, international, emerging markets), and different asset classes (stocks, bonds, real estate, commodities)[cite: 37].

## Bond Markets and Fixed Income Mechanics

A bond is a fixed income instrument that represents a loan made by an investor to a borrower (typically corporate or governmental)[cite: 37]. Bonds are used by companies, municipalities, states, and sovereign governments to finance projects and operations[cite: 37]. The contract governing a bond issue is called an indenture, specifying par value, coupon frequency, and maturity date.

Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions[cite: 37]. When interest rates rise, existing bond prices fall (and their yields rise)[cite: 37]. When interest rates fall, existing bond prices rise (and their yields fall)[cite: 37]. This inverse relationship is fundamental to fixed income investing[cite: 37]. 

Fixed income risk assessment relies heavily on yield to maturity (YTM), Macaulay duration, and modified duration. Duration quantifies the sensitivity of a bond's price to changes in interest rates, expressed in years. A portfolio duration of 6 indicates that a 100-basis-point (1.0%) increase in interest rates results in an approximate 6% decline in portfolio market value. Convexity accounts for the curvature of the price-yield relationship, providing a second-order risk adjustment. Credit risk spreads evaluate the yield premium demanded by investors over benchmark sovereign risk-free treasuries to compensate for default probability.

## Financial Ratios and Fundamental Valuation

Financial ratios extract standardized insight from balance sheets, income statements, and cash flow reports to evaluate solvency, operating efficiency, and relative valuation.

Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio: The P/E ratio measures a company's current share price relative to its earnings per share (EPS)[cite: 37]. A high P/E ratio might mean that a stock's price is high relative to earnings, and possibly overvalued[cite: 37]. Alternatively, it might indicate that investors expect high growth[cite: 37]. Analysts distinguish between trailing P/E (historical twelve-month earnings) and forward P/E (projected twelve-month earnings consensus).

Return on Equity (ROE): ROE is a measure of financial performance calculated by dividing net income by shareholders' equity[cite: 37]. It measures how effectively management is using a company's assets to generate profits[cite: 37]. DuPont Analysis decomposes ROE into net profit margin, asset turnover, and financial leverage to isolate operational drivers.

Debt-to-Equity (D/E) Ratio: The D/E ratio compares a company's total liabilities to its shareholder equity[cite: 37]. It is used to evaluate a company's financial leverage[cite: 37]. A high D/E ratio indicates that a company has been aggressive in financing its growth with debt[cite: 37].

Liquidity and Cash Flow Metrics: The Current Ratio (Current Assets / Current Liabilities) and Quick Ratio ((Cash + Marketable Securities + Receivables) / Current Liabilities) assess short-term insolvency risk. Enterprise Value to EBITDA (EV/EBITDA) normalizes capital structure differences across firms by including market equity, total debt, and minority interest minus cash reserves. Free Cash Flow (FCF), defined as Operating Cash Flow minus Capital Expenditures, determines discretionary capital available for debt amortization, dividend distributions, share repurchases, or acquisitions.

## Cryptocurrency and Digital Assets

Cryptocurrency is a digital or virtual currency that is secured by cryptography, which makes it nearly impossible to counterfeit or double-spend[cite: 37]. Bitcoin, created in 2009, was the first cryptocurrency and remains the largest by market capitalization[cite: 37].

Blockchain technology underlies most cryptocurrencies[cite: 37]. A blockchain is a distributed ledger enforced by a disparate network of computers[cite: 37]. Transactions are recorded in "blocks" and chained together chronologically[cite: 37]. Network consensus models prevent double-spending without central clearinghouses. Proof of Work (PoW) demands cryptographic hashing computation to secure chain state, whereas Proof of Stake (PoS) establishes validator authority proportional to economic tokens locked into protocol staking contracts.

DeFi (Decentralized Finance) refers to financial applications built on blockchain networks that aim to recreate and improve upon traditional financial systems without the need for intermediaries like banks[cite: 37]. Automated Market Makers (AMMs) use algorithmic liquidity pool equations ($x \cdot y = k$) instead of centralized order books to execute decentralized token swaps, while over-collateralized lending protocols enable automated algorithmic credit markets.

## Inflation and Monetary Policy

Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices for goods and services rises, and subsequently, purchasing power falls[cite: 37]. Central banks attempt to limit inflation — and avoid deflation — in order to keep the economy running smoothly[cite: 37]. Economists track inflation using indices like the Consumer Price Index (CPI) and Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE). Demand-pull inflation occurs when aggregate macroeconomic demand outpaces aggregate productive capacity; cost-push inflation arises from sudden aggregate supply shocks, such as spikes in raw energy and commodity inputs.

The Federal Reserve (Fed) uses monetary policy tools — primarily the federal funds rate — to control inflation[cite: 37]. When inflation is high, the Fed raises interest rates to slow economic activity[cite: 37]. When recession threatens, the Fed cuts rates to stimulate borrowing and spending[cite: 37]. The yield curve maps yields across standard treasury maturities (3 months to 30 years). A yield curve inversion—wherein short-term rates exceed long-term yields—consistently serves as a macroeconomic indicator of market expectations for monetary deceleration and impending recession.

## Quantitative Easing (QE) and Balance Sheet Operations

Quantitative easing is a form of unconventional monetary policy in which a central bank purchases longer-term securities from the open market in order to increase the money supply and encourage lending and investment[cite: 37]. Buying these securities adds new money to the economy and also serves to lower interest rates[cite: 37].

The Federal Reserve implemented multiple rounds of QE following the 2008 financial crisis and again during the COVID-19 pandemic to stabilize financial markets and support economic recovery[cite: 37]. The mechanism functions by expanding the central bank's asset base via direct purchases of long-dated sovereign debt and agency mortgage-backed securities (MBS), injecting liquidity directly into member bank reserve accounts. 

Conversely, Quantitative Tightening (QT) reduces central bank balance sheet assets by allowing securities to mature without reinvestment, absorbing excess commercial liquidity and elevating long-duration borrowing costs across corporate credit channels.

## Derivatives and Risk Hedging

Financial derivatives derive contractual value from underlying reference assets, such as equities, commodities, interest rates, or currency pairs. Key derivative instruments include futures, forwards, options, and swaps.

Futures and Forwards: Futures are standardized, exchange-traded contracts requiring delivery or cash settlement of an underlying asset at a specified future date and strike price. Daily mark-to-market margins mitigate counterparty credit risk. Forwards are custom, over-the-counter (OTC) agreements exposed to direct bilateral counterparty default.

Options Contracts: Call options grant holders the right, but not the obligation, to purchase underlying assets at a strike price before or upon expiration; put options grant the right to sell. Option sensitivity parameters (The Greeks) quantify structural exposure: Delta measures price change relative to underlying asset shifts, Gamma measures rate of Delta change, Theta quantifies daily time decay, and Vega tracks sensitivity to implied volatility. Institutional asset managers employ derivatives to execute covered call strategies, tail-risk portfolio insurance, or interest rate swaps that transform floating-rate debt into fixed liabilities.
