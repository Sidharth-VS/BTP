## Contract Law and Remedies

A contract is a legally enforceable agreement between two or more parties that creates mutual obligations[cite: 38]. For a contract to be valid, it must contain: offer, acceptance, consideration, mutual assent (meeting of the minds), capacity, and legality[cite: 38].

Offer and Acceptance: An offer is a proposal made by one party (the offeror) to another (the offeree), indicating a willingness to enter into a contract on certain terms[cite: 38]. Acceptance is the offeree's agreement to those terms[cite: 38]. Under the common-law mirror image rule, an acceptance must match the offer terms exactly; counteroffers operate as rejections of the initial offer. Under the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC Section 2-207), merchants can form contracts even with variant terms.

Consideration is something of value exchanged between the parties[cite: 38]. It distinguishes a contract from a gift[cite: 38]. Consideration can be money, goods, services, or a promise to perform or refrain from an action (forbearance)[cite: 38]. Past consideration or pre-existing legal duties cannot support an enforceable contract.

The Statute of Frauds mandates written instruments signed by the bound party for specific agreements: real property transfers, agreements impossible to perform within one year, promises answering for debt defaults (suretyship), and UCC goods transactions exceeding $500. When breach occurs, courts classify it as material (excusing non-breaching performance and permitting termination) or minor. Legal remedies primarily include expectation damages to place the injured party in the position expected upon full performance, consequential damages foreseeable under the rule of Hadley v. Baxendale, and liquidated damages pre-agreed in the contract text.

## Tort Law and Civil Liability

A tort is a civil wrong that causes harm or injury, leading to legal liability[cite: 38]. Unlike criminal law, tort law focuses on compensating the injured party rather than punishing the wrongdoer[cite: 38].

Negligence is the most common type of tort[cite: 38]. To establish negligence, a plaintiff must prove[cite: 38]:
1. Duty: The defendant owed a duty of care to the plaintiff (defined by the objective standard of a reasonably prudent person under like circumstances)[cite: 38]
2. Breach: The defendant breached that duty[cite: 38]
3. Causation: The breach caused the plaintiff's injury[cite: 38]. This requires both actual cause ("but-for" causation) and proximate cause (the injury was a foreseeable consequence within the scope of liability)
4. Damages: The plaintiff suffered actual damages[cite: 38]

Comparative negligence regimes apportion damages based on fault percentages. Pure comparative fault permits recovery even if the plaintiff is 90% responsible; modified comparative regimes bar recovery if plaintiff fault exceeds 50% or 51%. 

Strict liability applies regardless of fault[cite: 38]. Product liability cases often involve strict liability — manufacturers can be held liable for defective products even without proof of negligence[cite: 38]. Defect categories include design defects, manufacturing deviations, and failures to warn (inadequate labels). Intentional torts require intentional harm, covering assault, battery, false imprisonment, intentional infliction of emotional distress, trespass, and conversion.

## Intellectual Property Frameworks

Intellectual property (IP) refers to creations of the mind — inventions, literary and artistic works, designs, and symbols[cite: 38]. IP rights are protected by law through patents, copyright, trademarks, and trade secrets[cite: 38].

Patents grant inventors exclusive rights to their inventions for a limited period (typically 20 years) in exchange for public disclosure[cite: 38]. There are utility patents (functional mechanisms), design patents (ornamental configurations, 15-year term), and plant patents[cite: 38]. Inventions must exhibit novelty, statutory utility, and non-obviousness over prior art to survive USPTO examination.

Copyright protects original works of authorship — literary, artistic, musical, and software works[cite: 38]. Copyright protection is automatic upon creation and lasts for the life of the author plus 70 years[cite: 38]. Exclusive rights include reproduction, derivative work adaptation, distribution, and public performance. The Fair Use doctrine provides an affirmative defense evaluated across four statutory factors: purpose and character of use, nature of the copyrighted work, amount used relative to the whole, and the effect of the use on the potential commercial market.

Trademarks protect brand names, logos, and slogans that distinguish goods or services[cite: 38]. Unlike patents and copyrights, trademark protection can last indefinitely as long as the mark is used in commerce[cite: 38]. Enforceability rests upon consumer confusion standards and distinctiveness spectra (fanciful, arbitrary, suggestive, descriptive, or generic).

Trade Secrets protect confidential commercial information conferring economic advantage, such as algorithms and proprietary chemical formulations. Governed under the Defend Trade Secrets Act (DTSA) and the Uniform Trade Secrets Act (UTSA), protection endures indefinitely provided the owner takes reasonable measures to maintain secrecy.

## Constitutional Law and Judicial Scrutiny

The U.S. Constitution is the supreme law of the United States[cite: 38]. The Bill of Rights (first 10 amendments) protects fundamental civil liberties — freedom of speech, religion, press, assembly, and petition (1st Amendment); right to bear arms (2nd Amendment); protections against unreasonable searches and seizures (4th Amendment); right against self-incrimination and double jeopardy (5th Amendment)[cite: 38].

Due process (5th and 14th Amendments) requires that the government must respect all legal rights owed to a person according to the law[cite: 38]. Substantive due process protects certain fundamental rights from government interference[cite: 38]. Procedural due process ensures fair procedures before deprivation of life, liberty, or property, evaluated under the balancing test of Mathews v. Eldridge (private interest, risk of erroneous deprivation, and government burden)[cite: 38].

Judicial review, established in Marbury v. Madison, empowers federal courts to strike down unconstitutional statutes. Courts evaluate state action under three standards of judicial scrutiny:
- Strict Scrutiny: Applied to suspect classifications (race, national origin) or fundamental rights burdens. The law must be narrowly tailored to serve a compelling governmental interest using the least restrictive means.
- Intermediate Scrutiny: Applied to quasi-suspect classifications (gender, commercial speech restrictions). The law must be substantially related to an important governmental objective.
- Rational Basis Review: Applied to general economic and social legislation. The law must be rationally related to a legitimate state interest, with strong presumptions of constitutionality.

## Criminal Law and Procedural Safeguards

Criminal law involves the prosecution by the government of a person for an act classified as a crime[cite: 38]. Crimes are divided into felonies (serious crimes, typically punishable by more than one year in prison) and misdemeanors (less serious crimes)[cite: 38].

The elements of a crime typically include: actus reus (the guilty act) and mens rea (the guilty mind or criminal intent)[cite: 38]. Both must be proven beyond a reasonable doubt for a conviction[cite: 38]. Mens rea categories codified in the Model Penal Code (MPC) span purposely, knowingly, recklessly, and negligently. Inchoate crimes criminalize non-completed acts, including attempt, solicitation, and conspiracy (which requires an agreement and an overt act in furtherance of the unlawful design).

Criminal defenses include: self-defense, insanity defense, alibi, duress, entrapment, and statute of limitations[cite: 38]. The Fourth Amendment exclusionary rule prevents illegally obtained evidence from being used in court[cite: 38]. Under the Fruit of the Poisonous Tree doctrine, secondary evidence discovered through tainted searches is excluded unless exceptions apply: independent source, inevitable discovery, or attenuated taint. The Fifth Amendment establishes Miranda warnings against custodial self-incrimination, while the Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to counsel, a speedy and public jury trial, and the right to confront accusers.

## Data Privacy and Regulatory Compliance

Data protection statutes regulate the automated collection, storage, transfer, and processing of personally identifiable information (PII).

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is a comprehensive EU regulation on data privacy that applies to all organizations processing the personal data of EU residents[cite: 38]. Key principles include[cite: 38]:
- Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency[cite: 38]
- Purpose limitation (data collected for specified, explicit purposes)[cite: 38]
- Data minimization[cite: 38]
- Accuracy[cite: 38]
- Storage limitation[cite: 38]
- Integrity and confidentiality[cite: 38]
- Accountability (mandating Data Protection Officers and Privacy Impact Assessments)

Data subjects hold the right to access, rectification, portability, and erasure ("the right to be forgotten"). Data controllers transferring personal data across international boundaries must implement valid cross-border instruments, such as Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs) or adequacy decisions. Non-compliance risks European Data Protection Board (EDPB) administrative fines up to 20 million euros or 4% of global annual turnover.

The California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) grants California residents rights over their personal information, including the right to know, right to delete, right to opt-out of sale, and right to non-discrimination[cite: 38].

HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) establishes national standards for protecting sensitive patient health information (PHI)[cite: 38]. Covered entities must implement safeguards to ensure the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of PHI[cite: 38]. The HIPAA Security Rule requires explicit administrative, physical, and technical safeguards, while the Breach Notification Rule mandates swift notification following unauthorized exposure of unencrypted PHI.

## Employment Law and Workplace Regulations

Employment law governs the rights and obligations between employers and employees[cite: 38]. Key areas include[cite: 38]:
- Title VII of the Civil Rights Act: prohibits employment discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin[cite: 38]. Claims proceed via disparate treatment (intentional discrimination) or disparate impact (facially neutral practices causing unjustified adverse selection).
- ADA (Americans with Disabilities Act): prohibits discrimination against qualified individuals with disabilities and mandates reasonable accommodations absent undue hardship on business operations[cite: 38].
- FLSA (Fair Labor Standards Act): establishes minimum wage, overtime pay (1.5x regular pay beyond 40 weekly hours), and child labor restrictions[cite: 38]. It distinguishes exempt executive/administrative employees from non-exempt employees entitled to statutory overtime.
- FMLA (Family and Medical Leave Act): provides eligible employees with unpaid, job-protected leave (up to 12 weeks annually) for qualifying medical or family events[cite: 38].

At-will employment (common in the U.S.) means either the employer or employee can terminate the employment relationship at any time, for any reason (with some exceptions), without liability[cite: 38]. Major statutory and common-law exceptions prohibit terminations that violate statutory non-discrimination acts, public policy (e.g., retaliation for whistleblowing or workers' compensation claims), or implied covenants of good faith and fair dealing.

## Civil Procedure and Litigation Stages

Civil litigation in common-law jurisdictions proceeds through structured procedural steps under codified frameworks, such as the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure (FRCP).

Jurisdiction and Pleadings: A court cannot adjudicate a dispute without both subject-matter jurisdiction (power over the class of claim, such as federal question or diversity jurisdiction) and personal jurisdiction (constitutional authority over the defendant, established through minimum contacts satisfying fair play and substantial justice). Litigation commences when the plaintiff files a complaint detailing allegations and prayers for relief, prompting the defendant's answer or motion to dismiss under FRCP 12(b)(6) for failure to state a legally cognizable claim.

Discovery and Summary Judgment: In discovery, litigants exchange evidence via depositions, interrogatories, document requests, and requests for admission. Under FRCP Rule 56, parties can move for summary judgment, which courts grant if there is no genuine dispute of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, avoiding unnecessary jury trials.
